# Drafting from the interview

## The reader contract

Answer these privately before writing, one sentence each:

- **Reader:** who has this question or problem?
- **So what:** what changes for them after reading: what they understand, decide or do?
- **Why this author:** what did the user do, decide, test or see firsthand that gives them standing?
- **Focus:** three to seven words. A compass for you, not a headline.

If one can't be answered without generic language, the piece isn't ready: narrow the angle, or ask the one question that's missing.

## Pick the structure the material fits

| Structure | Fits | Order |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First-person case | Something they did and what it showed | Situation or direct claim, what they did or saw, the evidence, the limits, what the reader can use |
| Argument | A view they hold | The point early, evidence and mechanism, the strongest objection from people who hold it, the conclusion |
| How-to | A method others can repeat | The outcome, what you need first, steps in order, where it fails |
| Explainer | A question readers keep asking | The question, a short answer, the parts in the order the reader needs them, the limits |
| Scene, then the point | One moment that holds the whole idea | The scene, why it matters (soon), evidence and context, a return to the scene with new meaning |

Interview order and article order can differ. Use the order the reader needs.

## Opening

- Make the point, or the promise, clear early. A real scene can come first if the point follows within a paragraph or two.
- Open with a question only if it's the reader's real question and the article answers it quickly.
- No throat-clearing: "In today's fast-paced world", "Let's dive in", "Have you ever wondered".

## Middle

- One job per section. If two sections do the same job, merge them.
- Put evidence next to the claim it supports, and a limit next to the claim it limits.
- Move from claim to concrete detail and back: a number, a decision, a moment, a document, then what it means.
- Make transitions show the relationship: contrast, cause, consequence, a shift in time.
- Quote only words the user actually said or wrote. A remembered conversation becomes paraphrase unless they have a record. If you translated a quote, say so.

## Ending

End on what the material earns: the consequence, the next decision, a return to the opening detail with new meaning, or an honest open question. No slogan, no closing moral the user didn't state, no recap of the whole piece, and no engagement prompt unless they ask for one.

## Their voice

- Use their words, phrases and terms, even where a synonym sounds smoother.
- Keep real uncertainty, stated once. Drop stacked qualifiers.
- Cut the generated layer unless the user said it: inflated significance ("a pivotal moment", "a game-changer"), hype adjectives, "not just X, but Y" formulas, lists of three made up for rhythm, signposting ("here's the thing", "let's unpack this"), one-line slogans.
- Don't add jokes, emojis, profanity or a persona to sound human. Keep the user's own only if the platform suits it.

## Format

- **LinkedIn article:** a specific headline that says what the reader gets, an opening that works without the post that shares it, short sections, headings only where they help navigation.
- **LinkedIn or social post:** one point, one example, and the point in the first line or two.
- **Blog post:** a headline and subheads that say what each section delivers; link the sources behind outside claims.
- **Newsletter:** open with why this reaches the reader now, only if there's a real reason.

Length follows the material. If the user names a length, meet it by cutting or by asking for more material, never with filler.

## Gaps and provenance

Mark every gap the draft can't fill from the user's material as `[TK: the exact question]`. Give each a fallback: cut the passage, or keep the plain version their material supports.

After the draft, add a short note:

- **From you:** the examples, numbers, decisions and phrases it uses.
- **Added:** structure, transitions, headline and subheads.
- **Open:** each `[TK]` with its fallback.

When they answer, replace the markers. A final version has none.

## Final check

- The headline and opening promise what the body delivers.
- Every paragraph passes "So what?" and "Could anyone else have written this?", short necessary context aside.
- No invented scene, quote, emotion, decision, result or number.
- Numbers keep their definition, baseline and period. Team results stay team results.
- Other people's names and confidential details appear only where the user cleared them.
- The ending pays off the opening.

## Fictional example

Everything below is invented. It shows the method, not facts to reuse.

The user's answer: "We had a weekly status meeting, nine people, an hour. I asked everyone to write three lines in a doc by Monday noon instead. Two people hated it at first. After a month we cancelled the meeting. I don't know if it saved time overall, people spent time writing."

Draft paragraph: "For a month, the nine of us swapped the Monday status hour for three written lines each, due by noon. Two people disliked it at first. By the end of the month, we cancelled the meeting. Whether it saved time overall, I can't say: writing the updates took time too."

Not allowed: "Cutting the meeting saved our team nine hours a week and transformed our culture." The user never measured time saved, and never said anything about culture.
