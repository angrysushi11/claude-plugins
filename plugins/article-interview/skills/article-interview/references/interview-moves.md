# Choosing the next question

A decision aid, not a script. Pick the question that recovers what the latest answer is missing. Use an example only when the answer supports its premise.

| The answer contains | What to recover | Example question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An abstraction: "It got much harder to trust the numbers." | One concrete instance | "What was the first number that made you doubt it?" |
| "Then I changed the setup." | The change itself | "What did you change?" |
| An action with no reasoning | What they knew at the time | "What made that seem like the right call then?" |
| "It was better after that." | What "better" means, before any cause | "What was different in the result?" |
| An event with an unclear order | The sequence | "What happened right before that?" |
| A process described in general terms | The actual step or artifact | "What did that step look like the first time you did it?" |
| A result with a number | Its baseline, period and source | "What was it before, and over what period?" |
| A persuasive general conclusion | Its limit | "Where has that worked less well?" |
| A topic with a standard playbook | Where they depart from it, and why | "What would you leave out of the usual advice on this?" |
| A firm view with no named opponent | The real objection, from people who hold it | "Who disagrees with you, and what's their best argument?" |
| A rich answer that covers several gaps | The most important gap left | Ask about that one; don't re-ask what's answered. |
| A vague example in an existing draft | Their actual instance | "This example could be anyone's. What's your version?" |

## Habits that keep answers true

- Don't presuppose. "How did removing the approval step speed things up?" assumes a diagnosis, a change and a result. Ask what happened first, then ask about causes.
- Ask about feelings only when the feeling matters to the piece. It is not a required stage.
- Use scene questions for a moment the reader needs to see, sequence questions when order explains a decision, and explanation questions when the value is a mechanism or a judgment. A practical article doesn't need drama.
- If an answer is short, make the next question smaller or follow one of their words. Don't answer it for them.
- If an answer is long, let it change the plan.
- Reflect their point back only at a turning point or where a misunderstanding would change the piece, not after every answer.
- Precise yes-or-no questions are fine for facts: "Was that the whole team or just your group?"
- If they can't remember, note it and narrow the claim. Don't push for a better memory.
